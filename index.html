Are Urban and Rural Drug Mortality Rates Converging Over Time?

Most often, the blame for the drug epidemic in the U.S. is placed on big cities and cross-border populations (especially the southern border). The assumption has always been that rural America is more insulated — that distance from urban centers offers some protection. But does the data support that? And what about the border?

This visualization uses the official 2023 CDC NCHS Urban-Rural classifications to compare the median estimated age-adjusted drug mortality rates between urban and rural counties across years. Scroll down to see the story unfold.

1999

The Initial Divide

Urban counties open at 5.3 deaths per 100k; rural counties at 3.9. The gap felt structural — distance from cities seemed to offer a natural buffer against addiction. The CDC later flagged this year as the official start of the opioid epidemic.

In the background

OxyContin (FDA-approved 1995) was being aggressively marketed by Purdue Pharma's 671-person sales force — more reps than the CDC's entire injury-prevention division. Sales reps were explicitly instructed to tell doctors the drug was safe from addiction.

"Purdue's sales reps were instructed to tell doctors it was 'virtually non-addictive.'"

— Beth Macy, Dopesick (2018), p. 28

2007

The $634 Million Admission

Rural rates are closing the gap fast. Yet the landmark legal reckoning that followed only accelerated substitution to new and more dangerous supply chains — the fine was absorbed as a cost of doing business.

May 10, 2007

Purdue Pharma LP and three top executives plead guilty to federal charges of misbranding OxyContin with intent to defraud. Total settlement: $634.5 million — at the time the largest criminal fine ever levied against a pharmaceutical company. No executives served prison time. Nationwide OxyContin prescribing continued to rise through 2010.

"These are not white-collar criminals who steal money; these are people who have caused people to die."

— Sen. Arlen Specter (PA), Senate Judiciary Committee, July 31, 2007

2010

The Crossover

Rural counties (11.3/100k) overtake urban (10.3/100k) for the first time. In the same year, a policy meant to reduce pill abuse instead redirected the epidemic — and deepened it. Rural communities, already lacking treatment infrastructure, bore the worst of this transition.

August 2010

The FDA approves a tamper-resistant, abuse-deterrent reformulation of OxyContin. The new formula converted to a gel when crushed or dissolved, blocking the fast-onset high that dependent users relied on. With pills no longer usable this way, many switched to heroin — which was cheaper, widely available, and could be injected for a similar effect. Researchers found states with higher pre-2010 OxyContin misuse saw the sharpest spikes in heroin deaths post-reformulation. Studies show 90% of new heroin users in the 2000s had started on prescription opioids.

2013

The Synthetic Wave

The epidemic enters its third, deadliest phase. Both rural and urban lines now track together — geography no longer predicts exposure to fatal overdose. A new, far more potent threat has entered the supply chain.

2013–2014

Illicit fentanyl — 50 to 100 times more potent than morphine — begins appearing at scale in the U.S. drug supply, first mixed into heroin, then pressed into counterfeit pills. The DEA flags it in its 2014 National Drug Threat Assessment as an emerging crisis.

"We know of no other medication routinely used for a noncancer condition that kills patients so frequently."

— CDC Director Tom Frieden, MMWR, July 2, 2013
Sources
  • DEA National Drug Threat Assessment 2014
  • CDC Director Frieden, MMWR, July 2, 2013
2015

Complete Convergence

Rural 15.4 per 100k. Urban 15.7 per 100k. The divide is gone. The epidemic is universal — no longer a rural story, no longer an urban story.

Federal response

January 2016: the Obama administration requests $1.1 billion in emergency funding — the largest single federal investment in opioid treatment to date. March 2016: the CDC releases its first opioid prescribing guidelines, twenty years after OxyContin launched. The Comprehensive Addiction and Recovery Act (CARA) is signed into law on July 22, 2016.

"The opioid epidemic does not discriminate. It affects rural counties and cities alike."

— Sens. Rob Portman (R-OH) & Sheldon Whitehouse (D-RI), CARA co-sponsors

Political Context · Regional Spotlight 2015

Drugs, the Border, and the Data

“They’re bringing drugs. They’re bringing crime” became the dominant frame — anchoring border enforcement as the primary response to the epidemic. But ~90% of fentanyl seized at the U.S.–Mexico border enters through legal ports of entry, primarily by U.S. citizens. And the 24 counties that actually line the border in CA, AZ, NM, and TX? They recorded some of the lowest mortality rates in the country.

    Conclusion

    The Geography Didn’t Matter — The Drug Did

    For decades, the dominant assumption was simple: cities breed addiction, and distance from them offers protection. Rural isolation was treated as a kind of natural insulation. The data says otherwise.

    From 1999 to 2015, rural counties didn’t stay protected — they caught up. By 2010 they briefly surpassed urban rates. By 2015 the lines had converged completely: rural at 15.4 per 100k, urban at 15.7. The gap had closed to statistical noise.

    The same pattern undermines the border narrative. The 24 counties that physically line the U.S.–Mexico border — repeatedly framed as the source of the crisis — posted some of the lowest mortality rates in the country. 18 of 24 fell below the national median.

    This epidemic was manufactured domestically, marketed by a pharmaceutical industry that lied about addiction risk, and amplified by policy failures at every level. When prescription pills became harder to obtain, heroin and fentanyl filled the gap — everywhere, regardless of how far you were from a city or a border.

    “The opioid epidemic does not discriminate. It affects rural counties and cities alike.”

    — Sens. Rob Portman (R-OH) & Sheldon Whitehouse (D-RI), CARA co-sponsors, 2016
